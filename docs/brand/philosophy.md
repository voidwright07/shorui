# Vermilion Restraint

*A visual philosophy of ink, paper, and one decisive mark.*

Vermilion Restraint starts from a sheet of paper and a few marks on it: ink lines, the white around them, and one small red seal. Everything else is taken out until the remaining shapes feel unavoidable, as if no other arrangement were possible. The work should read as an object that has been weighed, measured and approved. It is quiet and exact, and it carries a single point of warmth. Each piece must look meticulously crafted, the product of deep expertise. The goal is the calm of something that took countless hours to get right and now hides that effort entirely.

Space is the main material. Large fields of flat, dark graphite or unbleached paper hold small, precise forms. Proportions come from paper itself. The √2 ratio of a standard sheet governs rectangles, margins and the gaps between elements, so the composition feels ruled and settled. Forms are perfect geometry: true rectangles, hairline rules, strokes of one constant weight with round ends. Nothing is drawn freehand and nothing is decorative. Every edge sits on a grid that the viewer senses but never sees, and placement is refined with painstaking attention until moving any element by a single unit would feel wrong.

Colour follows a strict economy of four: deep ink, warm paper, a dull charcoal ground, and vermilion. Vermilion is the only chromatic note, and it always plays the same role: the seal on the document, the signature that makes the paper count. It occupies the smallest area of any element, and the eye should land on it last and stay there. Surfaces are flat, with no gradients, glows or shadows. Depth comes only from value steps and hairline borders. Material honesty matters: paper should feel like paper because of its exact warm off-white, not because of a texture filter.

Rhythm comes from repeated vertical strokes, set the way columns of text run down a page and are read right to left. Lengths vary like real sentences: one runs the full measure, one stops short, one ends early. The repetition invites slow viewing. A systematic layer sits around the work: a faint unit grid, small reference numerals, dimension ticks and plate numbers, so each composition reads like a specimen from a careful discipline that classifies documents. That apparatus is drawn at the lowest possible volume, and it should look like master-level draughting, never like a template.

Text is rare, small and deliberate. Labels use a thin, wide-set monospace and appear only where they anchor what the form already says. Occasionally a single word is set large as a typographic object, and when it is, it follows the vertical reading order and has room on every side. Hierarchy is decided by scale and isolation, never by weight or colour: the main form is very large, the apparatus is very small, and the space between them is generous. Nothing overlaps and nothing crowds the edge. Margins are wide and consistent.

The finished artifact should feel museum-ready: crisp at every size, balanced so precisely that it seems to have arrived complete, and carrying the authority of craft at the highest level. When the work is reduced to a few pixels, it should still read as paper, ink and seal. That ability to survive reduction is the final test.
